Fifth Species Counterpoint Ñ Operational Guide (Florid Counterpoint)I. General Definition and ScopeFifth-species counterpoint consists of a strictly regulated synthesis of all rhythmic and contrapuntal procedures admitted in first-, second-, third-, and fourth-species counterpoint within a single exercise. This guide defines the conditions under which such a synthesis is to be constructed, evaluated, and validated.The fifth species functions as a synthetic species, not as an autonomous rule system. All general voice-leading rules from previous species and guides remain fully in force throughout, except where explicitly modified in the present guide.No new categories of consonance, dissonance, or voice-leading motion are introduced.All musical events must be derivable from rules already validated in the earlier species.1. The strong beat is defined as the first beat of the measure, and the weak beat as the second beat of the measure.2. The cantus firmus is written in whole notes throughout and cannot be modified.3. The counterpoint may combine:o whole notes (1:1),o half notes (2:1),o quarter notes (4:1),o eighth notes (8:1),o tied half notes forming syncopations.4. Mixed rhythmic values within a single measure are permitted.II. Metric Framework and Rhythmic OrganizationThe binary metric hierarchy is the key compoment on the writing of the fifth-species as the choice of the species to write depends to the local metrical context of the progression. A. Strong Beats 1. Metrically strong beats constitute structural points of stability. As a general rule, notes placed on strong beats must form a consonant interval with the cantus firmus, except in the specific context of a suspension.2. The admissibility and function of notes on strong beats depend on the species logic activated by their rhythmic value.a. First Species logic (whole note on a strong beat)o First-species counterpoint coincides exclusively with strong beats, since the cantus firmus is articulated in whole notes.o A whole note on a strong beat must therefore always form a consonant interval with the cantus firmus.o No dissonance is admissible in this context.b. Second Species logic (half note on a strong beat)o In second species, the note placed on the strong beat must always be consonant.o Dissonance on the strong beat is categorically forbidden in this species.o The consonant must respect all traditional voice-leading constraints (motion categories, avoidance of parallels, spacing).c. Third Species logic (quarter-note writing on a strong beat)o In third species, quarter-note writing on a strong beat must be evaluated as part of a binary unit.o The first quarter note of the binary unit, which coincides with the strong beat, must always be consonant.o Consequently, third-species usage on a strong beat necessarily yields a consonance ? dissonance pattern within the unit, since a dissonance ? consonance pattern is not admissible at a strong beat.o Any dissonance associated with this pattern must occur on the weak subdivision and must resolve according to third-species rules.d. Fourth Species logic (suspension on a strong beat)o The only admissible dissonance on a strong beat occurs within the context of a suspension.o This dissonance must result exclusively from metric displacement of a consonant preparation on the preceding weak beat.o The suspended dissonance must resolve by descending step to a consonant interval.o Upward resolution on a strong beat is forbidden.3. No other type of dissonance is permitted on a strong beat. In particular, accented passing tones and non-prepared dissonances are strictly prohibited.B. Weak Beats1. Weak beats may host consonant or dissonant intervals, but their function and admissibility depend on the rhythmic value and the species logic activated by that value.a. Half note on a weak beat (Second Species logic)o A half note on a weak beat does not function as first species, since first-species counterpoint always coincides with a strong beat.o In this position, the half note is evaluated under second-species logic.o It must therefore form a consonant interval with the cantus firmus and comply with all traditional voice-leading constraints.b. Quarter-note writing on a weak beat (Third Species logic, binary subdivision)o Quarter-note writing on a weak beat must be evaluated as a binary unit spanning the beat.o The two quarter notes cannot be assessed independently, as a single quarter note would be metrically incomplete.o Within this binary unit:* the pattern consonance ? dissonance may occur, provided the dissonance arises by stepwise motion and belongs to a continuous melodic line;* the pattern dissonance ? consonance is admissible only on a weak beat, where the dissonance functions as a passing or diminutive event and resolves by step within the same beat.o In all cases, the following strong beat must be consonant, ensuring structural stability and allowing the activation of a new rhythmic or species logic.c. Consonant preparation for syncopation (Fourth Species logic)o A consonance on a weak beat may function as a preparation for syncopation.o In this case, the note must:* form a consonant interval with the cantus firmus,* be eligible for rhythmic continuation (tie) into the following strong beat,* allow for a metrically displaced dissonance and a descending stepwise resolution on the strong beat.o This preparation activates fourth-species logic and must be evaluated with respect to its future suspension behavior, not only its immediate consonant status.2. Any dissonance occurring on a weak beat is permitted only if it conforms to one of the following inherited models:o passing dissonance (second-species logic),o stepwise diminution or ornamentation (third-species logic).3. Consequently, any dissonant weak-beat note must:o be approached by step,o be left by step,o belong to a continuous melodic connection between consonant notes,o resolve to a consonance within the same durational span as the dissonant value, according to its rhythmic value:* a dissonant quarter note must resolve within the same beat,* a dissonant half note must resolve on the following strong beat.4. No leap may approach or leave a dissonant weak-beat note.5. Any dissonance not falling under the models listed above is forbidden.V. Dynamic Species Selection as a Decision ProtocolIn fifth-species counterpoint, rhythm, pitch, and species logic are evaluated jointly at every step, and none of these parameters may be selected in isolation. The generative process unfolds through incremental constraint satisfaction: metric position limits the rhythmic options available at each moment, voice-leading rules continuously filter the resulting rhythmÐpitch combinations, and viable solutions are gradually retained or discarded as the counterpoint progresses. Species choice is therefore not fixed in advance but emerges dynamically from this ongoing process of evaluation and selection.1. Metric Position IdentificationFor each new note, the system first determines the metric position (strong beat or weak beat). This determination constrains which rhythmic values and species logics may be activated at that point, in accordance with the rules governing strong and weak beats.2. Joint Generation of RhythmÐPitch CandidatesGiven the metric position, the system generates joint (rhythm, pitch) candidates. A candidate is admissible only if the pitch lies within a valid melodic range, forms a potentially legal interval with the cantus firmus, and does not immediately violate any voice-leading constraint (such as forbidden repetition, illegal leap, or improper spacing). If no pitch can realize a given rhythmic option without violation, that option is excluded.3. Local Validation and Progressive Constraint FilteringAt each step, all admissible joint candidates are evaluated for local voice-leading correctness. Candidates that satisfy both metric constraints and voice-leading rules remain viable, indicating that a locally coherent solution exists at that temporal point. The system then advances to the next temporal point (the following beat or subdivision), where the same evaluation is repeated. When conflicts arise, the set of viable candidates is progressively narrowed, eliminating choices that lead to impasses in later continuation.4. Commitment and BacktrackingIf no viable candidate remains at a given point, the system must return to the most recent decision point and attempt an alternative joint (rhythm, pitch) choice. Backtracking operates on the combined rhythmÐpitch decision, rather than on rhythm or pitch alone, ensuring that the generative process remains coherent and responsive to emerging constraints.VI. Special Cases in Fifth Species: Syncopation and Expanded Fourth-Species TreatmentThis section describes special cases specific to fifth-species counterpoint, in which fourth-species logic is retained but expanded through rhythmic realizations that do not occur in isolated fourth-species exercises.A. General PrincipleSyncopation remains governed by fourth-species logic but admits expanded rhythmic realizations in fifth species. A syncopation still consists of:¥ a consonant preparation,¥ a metrically displaced dissonance (when applicable),¥ a resolution governed by stepwise motion.No syncopation may violate these structural components.B. Preparation1. The preparation must always form a consonant interval with the cantus firmus.2. The preparation is typically a half note, but in fifth species it may serve as the source for varied rhythmic continuations, provided that its consonant and preparatory function is preserved.C. Treatment on the Strong BeatIn fifth species, the preparation does not necessarily reappear as a tied half note on the strong beat. Two realizations are permitted:Classical fourth-species realization: The preparation is tied into the strong beat as a half note.Division into two quarter notes on the strong beat: The first quarter note must be identical in pitch to the preparation and therefore forms the suspension dissonance and is tied to the prior half note. The second quarter note may serve one of the following functions:¥ resolution by descending stepwise motion,¥ anticipation, understood as repetition of the pitch of the upcoming resolution on the following weak beat,¥ consonant accented skip to a consonant interval above or below, not exceeding a perfect fifth, provided that the actual resolution occurs on the subsequent weak beat and that all voice-leading principles are respected.In all cases involving dissonance: the resolution must occur by descending step, upward resolution remains forbidden.D. Consonant SyncopationIf the metrically displaced note forms a consonant interval on the strong beat, no obligatory resolution is required, and the line may proceed freely, subject to general melodic and rhythmic constraints.VII. Eighth-Note Groupings and Ornamental RepetitionThis section describes an ornamental extension specific to fifth species, combining fourth- and third-species logic in a manner not prescribed in their isolated forms. In syncopation contexts, a grouping of two eighth notes may function as an ornamental elaboration of the suspensionÐresolution process, replacing the second quarter note on the strong beat, provided that the following conditions are met:¥ the structural resolution of the suspension remains a descending stepwise motion to a consonant interval,¥ the eighth-note pair does not redefine the point of resolution, but decorates either the resolution pitch itself or the approach to that resolution,¥ the ornamental figure remains strictly conjunct.A. Ornamented AccentIf the preparation (e.g., D as a half note) is tied into a quarter note forming the suspension dissonance, the subsequent eighth-note figure (e.g., EÐD) may precede the consonant resolution pitch (e.g., C as a quarter note).In this case:¥ the eighth-note ascent functions as an ornamented accent,¥ the true resolution of the syncopation remains the following consonant pitch,¥ abstracting away the ornament, the suspension is interpreted as resolving downward without interruption.B. Ornamented AnticipationIf the eighth-note pair decorates the pitch of the upcoming resolution (e.g., DÐCÐBÐC), the lower neighbor functions as an ornamental anticipation.In this case:¥ the resolution pitch is already implied metrically,¥ the eighth-note figure elaborates the weak-beat resolution,¥ the suspension remains structurally descending, despite surface ornamentation.In both cases, the suspension is always interpreted as descending at the structural level, the eighth-note grouping functions as an ornamental intensification rather than a deviation from fourth-species logic, and melodic continuity is not considered interrupted.VIII. Melodic Conduct and Line Priority1. Stepwise motion is normative.2. Leaps are permitted only between consonant notes.3. Any leap must be compensated by stepwise motion in the opposite direction.4. Chains of leaps in the same direction are forbidden.5. Melodic tritones (augmented fourths or diminished fifths) are forbidden.6. Pitch repetition is avoided except when rhythmically justified (eighth-note broderies, anticipations).The rhythmic variety of fifth species must always serve melodic coherence, not obscure it.IX. Beginning, Penultimate Measure, and Final CadenceA. Beginning1. The counterpoint may begin with a rest.2. The first sounding note must form a consonance with the cantus firmus.3. Perfect consonances are required at the opening (unison or octave; fifth only if the counterpoint is above).B. Penultimate MeasureThe last note of the penultimate measure must prepare the final consonance according to standard cadential patterns inherited from earlier species:* counterpoint above the cantus:o major sixth resolving to an octave,* counterpoint below the cantus:o minor third resolving to a unison.Syncopated cadential suspensions are preferred when possible.C. Final Measure1. The final note must be a whole note.2. The final interval must be a perfect consonance (unison or octave).X. Forbidden Successions and Voice-Leading ConstraintsAll prohibitions from first species apply without exception:* parallel fifths and octaves,* hidden fifths and octaves,* voice crossing,* excessive spacing,* leaps into or out of unison (except at beginning and end).The presence of intervening diminutions or ornamental figures does not neutralize forbidden parallels if they remain perceptible under reduction.Please check at the end if all those rules are correct and if not correct yourself.
