Fifth Species Counterpoint : Operational Guide (Florid Counterpoint) — v1.9
I. General Definition and Scope
Fifth-species counterpoint consists of a strictly regulated synthesis of all rhythmic and contrapuntal procedures admitted in first-, second-, third-, and fourth-species counterpoint within a single exercise. This guide defines the conditions under which such a synthesis is to be constructed, evaluated, and validated.
The fifth species functions as a synthetic species, not as an autonomous rule system. All general voice-leading rules from previous species and guides remain fully in force throughout, except where explicitly modified in the present guide.
No new categories of consonance, dissonance, or voice-leading motion are introduced.
All musical events must be derivable from rules already validated in the earlier species.
1. In a four beats measure, beats 1 and 3 are defined as strong beats, beats 2 and 4 are defined as weak beats.
2. The cantus firmus is written in whole notes throughout and cannot be modified.
3. The counterpoint may combine:
o whole notes (1:1),
o half notes (2:1),
o quarter notes (4:1),
o eighth notes (8:1),
o tied half notes forming syncopations.
4. Mixed rhythmic values within a single measure are permitted. A measured degree of rhythmic variety must be implemented in order to prevent the excessive repetition of identical rhythmic patterns. However, it must be explicitly stated that rhythmic variety does not authorize any deviation from the governing contrapuntal rules. All rules remain binding and must be strictly observed at all times. The rhythmic variety of fifth species must always serve melodic coherence, not obscure it.
II. Consonance Classification (reference for all interval judgments in this guide)
- Perfect consonances: unison, perfect 5th, perfect octave.
- Imperfect consonances: major 3rd, minor 3rd, major 6th, minor 6th, major 10th, minor 10th.
- Dissonances (all other intervals): seconds, perfect 4th, diminished 5th, tritone (augmented 4th), sevenths, and all compounds of these with the octave.
CRITICAL — The perfect 4th (P4) is ALWAYS dissonant in two-voice counterpoint, regardless of which voice is higher. When writing below the cantus firmus, do not confuse the P4 with a P5: if the lower note is a 4th below the upper note, the interval is a P4 and it is dissonant. For example, A3 below D4 = P4 (dissonant), not P5. A P5 below D4 would be G3. Always count intervals from the lower note upward to the upper note.
FORBIDDEN P4 PITCHES — When writing below the CF, the following counterpoint pitches create a dissonant P4 and must NEVER appear on a strong beat (beat 1 or 3) unless part of a valid tied suspension:
  CF = C4 → forbidden P4 below: G3 (G3–C4 = P4)
  CF = D4 → forbidden P4 below: A3 (A3–D4 = P4)
  CF = E4 → forbidden P4 below: B3 (B3–E4 = P4)
  CF = F4 → forbidden P4 below: C3 or C4 (C–F = P4)
  CF = G4 → forbidden P4 below: D3 or D4 (D–G = P4)
  CF = A3 → forbidden P4 below: E3 (E3–A3 = P4)
  CF = A4 → forbidden P4 below: E3 or E4 (E–A = P4)
Before placing any note on beat 1 or beat 3, check this table. If the pitch appears in the "forbidden" column for the current CF note, choose a different pitch.
When interval quality is not specified, the diatonic pitches of the mode govern the quality automatically. Accidentals are applied only where explicitly required by the cadential accidentals table or by the Bb rule for F (see §VII.5).
III. Metric Framework and Rhythmic Organization
A correct identification of the beat number is the key component in the writing of the fifth-species as the choice of the species to write depends on the local metrical context of the progression.
o Beats 1 and 3 constitute structural points of stability. As a general rule, notes placed on 1 and 3 must form a consonant interval with the cantus firmus, except in the specific context mentioned thereafter.
o Beats 2 and 4 may host consonant or dissonant intervals, but their function and admissibility depend on the rhythmic value and the species logic activated by that value.
o The admissibility and function of notes on strong beats depend on the species logic activated by their rhythmic value.
a. First Species logic
o First-species counterpoint coincides exclusively with beat 1, since the cantus firmus is articulated in whole notes.
o A whole note on a strong beat must therefore always form a consonant interval with the cantus firmus.
o No dissonance is admissible in this context.
o This rhythmic configuration, consisting exclusively of whole-note values, occurs only in the final bars of the fifth species.
b. Second Species logic
o In second species, the note placed on the strong beat must always be consonant.
o Dissonance on beat 1 is categorically forbidden in this species. Fux does not use any passing half note on beat 3, therefore they are also prohibited.
o The consonant must respect all traditional voice-leading constraints (motion categories, avoidance of parallels, spacing).
o A half note on beat 2 or 4 is strictly forbidden.
o In the fifth species, Fux does not use more than two distinct half notes after each other.
o No pitch repetition is allowed
c. Third Species logic (quarter-note writing)
o The first quarter note on beat 1 must be always consonant.
o Consequently, third-species usage follows a stepwise motion logic for the following pattern consonance → dissonance → consonance  pattern, either on beat 1, 2, 3 or 3, 4, 1.
o A dissonance is possible on beat 3 but must be followed by a consonance by step on beat 4. This creates the stepwise pattern on each beat of the measure consonance (beat 1) → dissonance (beat 2) → dissonance (beat 3) → consonance  (beat 4).
o The motion of two consonances after each other can in third species logic happen on every beat by step or by leap as long as it does not create voice leading errors.
d. Fourth Species logic
o Preparation (beat 3): A consonant half note on beat 3 is tied across the barline to beat 1 of the next measure.
o Suspension (beat 1): The tied note, now metrically displaced onto the strong beat, may form either a consonance or a dissonance with the cantus firmus. This is the only context in which a dissonance on beat 1 is admissible.
o Resolution: The suspended note must resolve by descending step to a consonant interval. Upward resolution is forbidden. The resolution occurs on beat 3 if the suspension is sustained until that point, or on beat 2 if intervening quarter-note diminution is introduced.
Any dissonance not falling under the models listed above is forbidden.
IV. Dynamic Species Selection as a Decision Protocol
In fifth-species counterpoint, rhythm, pitch, and species logic are evaluated jointly at every step, and none of these parameters may be selected in isolation. The generative process unfolds through incremental constraint satisfaction: metric position limits the rhythmic options available at each moment, voice-leading rules continuously filter the resulting rhythm-pitch combinations, and viable solutions are gradually retained or discarded as the counterpoint progresses. Species choice is therefore not fixed in advance but emerges dynamically from this ongoing process of evaluation and selection.
1. Metric Position Identification
For each new note, the system first determines the metric position (beat 1 or 2 or 3 or 4). This determination constrains which rhythmic values and species logics may be activated at that point, in accordance with the rules governing beats 1, 2, 3 and 4.
2. Joint Generation of Rhythm-Pitch Candidates
Given the metric position, the system generates joint (rhythm, pitch) candidates. A candidate is admissible only if the pitch lies within a valid melodic range, forms a potentially legal interval with the cantus firmus, and does not immediately violate any voice-leading constraint (such as forbidden repetition, illegal leap, or improper spacing). If no pitch can realize a given rhythmic option without violation, that option is excluded.
3. Local Validation and Progressive Constraint Filtering
At each step, all admissible joint candidates are evaluated for local voice-leading correctness. Candidates that satisfy both metric constraints and voice-leading rules remain viable, indicating that a locally coherent solution exists at that temporal point. The system then advances to the next temporal point (the following beat or subdivision), where the same evaluation is repeated. When conflicts arise, the set of viable candidates is progressively narrowed, eliminating choices that lead to impasses in later continuation.
4. Commitment and Backtracking
If no viable candidate remains at a given point, the system must return to the most recent decision point and attempt an alternative joint (rhythm, pitch) choice. Backtracking operates on the combined rhythm-pitch decision, rather than on rhythm or pitch alone, ensuring that the generative process remains coherent and responsive to emerging constraints.
V. Special Cases in Fifth Species: Syncopation and Expanded Fourth-Species Treatment
This section describes special cases specific to fifth-species counterpoint, in which fourth-species logic is retained but expanded through rhythmic realizations that do not occur in isolated fourth-species exercises.
A. General Principle
Syncopation remains governed by fourth-species logic but admits expanded rhythmic realizations in fifth species. A syncopation still consists of:
- a consonant preparation,
- a metrically displaced dissonance (when applicable),
- a resolution governed by stepwise motion.
No syncopation may violate these structural components.
B. Preparation
1. The preparation must always form a consonant interval with the cantus firmus.
2. The preparation is typically a half note, but in fifth species it may serve as the source for varied rhythmic continuations, provided that its consonant and preparatory function is preserved.
C. Treatment of the dissonance
In fifth species, the preparation does not necessarily reappear as a tied half note on the strong beat. Two realizations are permitted:
1. Classical fourth-species realization: The preparation is tied into the first beat as a half note.
2. Division\diminution into two quarter notes on the beat 1 and 2:
The first quarter note must be identical in pitch to the preparation and therefore forms the suspension dissonance and is tied to the prior half note.
The second quarter note may serve one of the following functions:
- resolution by descending stepwise motion,
- anticipation, understood as repetition of the pitch of the upcoming resolution on the following weak beat,
- consonant accented skip to a consonant interval above or below, not exceeding a perfect fifth, provided that the actual resolution occurs on the subsequent weak beat and that all voice-leading principles are respected.
In all cases involving dissonance: the resolution must occur by descending step. Upward resolution remains forbidden.

When the cantus firmus is placed above: the only permitted dissonant intervals between the cantus firmus and the generated voice are: a 2nd resolving to a 3rd, a 4th resolving to a 5th, and a 9th resolving to a 10th.
When the cantus firmus is placed below: the admissible suspension patterns are 4-3 and 7-6.

BELOW-CF SUSPENSION TABLE (intervals measured from lower note upward):
When writing BELOW the cantus firmus, the suspended note is in the lower voice. The intervals are measured from the counterpoint (lower) up to the CF (upper):
  • 2-3 suspension: the tied lower note forms a 2nd with the CF on beat 1, then resolves DOWN by step to a 3rd. Example: CF = D4; counterpoint C3 (preparation, consonant) → C3 tied to beat 1 = m2 against CF (dissonant) → resolves down to B2 = m3 against D4 (consonant).
  • 4-5 suspension: the tied lower note forms a 4th with the CF on beat 1, then resolves DOWN by step to a 5th. Example: CF = G4; counterpoint D3 (preparation) → D3 tied to beat 1 = P4 against G4 (dissonant) → resolves down to C3 = P5 against G4 (consonant).
  • 9-10 suspension: the tied lower note forms a 9th with the CF on beat 1, then resolves DOWN by step to a 10th. Example: CF = E4; counterpoint D3 (preparation) → D3 tied to beat 1 = M9 against E4 (dissonant) → resolves down to C3 = M10 against E4 (consonant).
In all below-CF suspensions, the counterpoint resolves DOWNWARD by step. The resolution always produces a larger interval number (2→3, 4→5, 9→10) because the lower voice descends further from the upper voice.

The 2-1 suspension is permitted only if the preparation forms a third in the preceding measure. The same principle applies to the 9-8 suspension, which is allowed only when the preparation forms a tenth in the preceding measure. This restriction exists to prevent delayed parallel octaves: if a 9-8 suspension were prepared by an 8th, the structural reduction would reveal an 8-8 succession, which is forbidden. By requiring a 10th as preparation, the reduction yields 10-8, which is legal. The LLM must apply this as a hard constraint and must not seek workarounds by re-labeling the preparation interval.

D. Consonant Syncopation
If a half note on beat 3 is tied to a consonant interval on beat 1 of the next measure, no obligatory resolution is required, and the line may proceed freely, subject to general voice leading and rhythmic constraints.
VI. Eighth-Note Groupings and Ornamental Repetition
This section describes an ornamental extension specific to fifth species, combining fourth- and third-species logic in a manner not prescribed in their isolated forms. During a syncopation, a grouping of two eighth notes may function as an ornamental elaboration of the suspension-resolution process, replacing the second quarter note on the strong beat, provided that the following conditions are met:
- the structural resolution of the suspension remains a descending stepwise motion to a consonant interval,
- the eighth-note pair does not redefine the point of resolution, but decorates either the resolution pitch itself or the approach to that resolution,
- the ornamental figure remains strictly conjunct.
A. Ornamented Accent
If the preparation (e.g., D as a half note) is tied into a quarter note forming the suspension dissonance, the subsequent eighth-note figure (e.g., E-D) may precede the consonant resolution pitch (e.g., C as a quarter note).
In this case:
- the eighth-note ascent functions as an ornamented accent,
- the true resolution of the syncopation remains the following consonant pitch,
- abstracting away the ornament, the suspension is interpreted as resolving downward without interruption.
B. Ornamented Anticipation
If the eighth-note pair decorates the pitch of the upcoming resolution (e.g., D-C-B-C), the lower neighbor functions as an ornamental anticipation.
In this case:
- the resolution pitch is already implied metrically,
- the eighth-note figure elaborates the weak-beat resolution,
- the suspension remains structurally descending, despite surface ornamentation.
In both cases, the suspension is always interpreted as descending at the structural level, the eighth-note grouping functions as an ornamental intensification rather than a deviation from fourth-species logic, and melodic continuity is not considered interrupted.
Eighth notes are not permitted in any context other than the prescribed cases of the ornamented accent and the ornamented anticipation. Groupings of four consecutive eighth notes must not be generated, as they do not conform to these rules and are inconsistent with Fux's compositional practice.
VII. Melodic Conduct and Line Priority
1. Stepwise motion is normative.
2. Leaps are permitted only between consonant notes.
3. Any leap must be compensated by stepwise motion in the opposite direction.
4. Melodic tritones (augmented fourths or diminished fifths) are forbidden.
5. Cantus Firmus in F (Bb Rule): When the cantus firmus is in F (Lydian mode), B must be lowered to Bb in the generated voice wherever it would create a melodic tritone. This applies in two specific situations:
   (a) Direct leap: if the generated voice leaps between F and B (or B and F), lower B to Bb to eliminate the augmented 4th.
   (b) Stepwise outline: if the generated voice moves stepwise through a passage whose outer boundary is a tritone — for example, the ascending line F–G–A–B or the descending line B–A–G–F — lower B to Bb to break the tritone frame.
   This inflection is not a blanket substitution of every B in the exercise; it applies wherever B participates in either of these two tritone-producing relationships with F. The rule applies to every rhythmic value — passing tones, preparation notes, suspension notes, and resolution notes included. Fux consistently uses this adjustment in all species when writing over the F cantus firmus.
6. Pitch repetition is avoided except when rhythmically justified for anticipations.
VIII. Beginning, Penultimate Measure, and Final Cadence
A. Beginning
1. The counterpoint may begin with a rest.
2. The first sounding note must form a consonance with the cantus firmus.
3. Perfect consonances are required at the opening (unison or octave; fifth only if the counterpoint is above).
B. Penultimate Measure
The penultimate measure must use a syncopated cadential suspension following fourth-species logic. This is not optional: Fux consistently employs a syncopated cadence in all fifth-species examples.
- Counterpoint above the cantus firmus: use a 7–6 suspension. The 6 (Major 6th) is the resolution and must be the last note of the penultimate measure, resolving to the octave in the final bar.
- Counterpoint below the cantus firmus: use a 2–3 suspension. The 3 (minor 3rd) is the resolution and must be the last note of the penultimate measure, resolving to the unison in the final bar.

CONCRETE CADENCE EXAMPLE — Counterpoint below CF in E:
  The CF for the last three measures is: M8 = E4, M9 = F4, M10 = E4.
  Step 1 — Choose preparation (M8, beat 3): Find a note consonant with E4 that will form a 2nd with F4 when tied into M9. The note E3 is consonant with E4 (P8) and E3 to F4 = m2 (a valid suspension interval below CF). Place E3 as a half note on beat 3 of M8.
  Step 2 — Suspension (M9, beat 1): E3 is tied across the barline. E3 to F4 = m2 (dissonant). This is a valid 2-3 suspension below the CF. ✓
  Step 3 — Resolution (M9, beat 3): E3 resolves DOWN by step to D3. D3 to F4 = m3 (compound) = minor 10th (consonant). D3 is the last sounding note of the penultimate measure. ✓
  Step 4 — Final (M10): E3 whole note. E3 to E4 = P8 (octave). ✓
  No cadential accidental is needed when the CF ends on E (the table says E / none).

RULE FOR BELOW-CF CADENCE: In the antepenultimate measure, place a consonant half note on beat 3 that will form a 2nd (or compound 2nd) with the CF of the penultimate measure when tied across the barline. Resolve downward by step on beat 3 of the penultimate measure to form a 3rd (or compound 3rd). The resolution note is the last sounding note before the final whole note.

The suspension is prepared by a consonant half note on beat 3 of the antepenultimate measure (the measure before the penultimate), tied across the barline to beat 1 of the penultimate measure (i.e., standard fourth-species ligature). The resolution falls on beat 3 of the penultimate measure, or earlier via quarter-note diminution, provided the structural resolution pitch (the Major 6th above or minor 3rd below) remains the last sounding note before the final bar.
Note on the 5th (generated voice above, cantus below): whenever a 5th appears in the penultimate measure — including as a quarter note on beat 2 within an ornamented syncopation — it must be a perfect 5th. When the penultimate CF note is B, the diatonic 5th above is F natural — a diminished 5th, which is forbidden. Apply F# to produce a perfect 5th. Example: cantus in A, penultimate CF note = B → any 5th in the counterpoint = F# (P5).
Apply the required accidental to the resolution note where it does not occur naturally:
Final note of CF / Required accidental on resolution note:
D / C#
C / none
E / none
F / none
G / F#
A / G#
C. Final Measure
1. The final note of the generated voice must be a whole note and must correspond with the last note of the cantus firmus voice. No further notes are allowed to be added.
2. The final interval must be a perfect consonance (unison or octave).
IX. Forbidden Successions and Voice-Leading Constraints
All prohibitions from first and second species apply without exception:
- Parallel fifths and octaves are forbidden. The presence of intervening diminutions or ornamental figures does not neutralize forbidden parallels if they remain perceptible under reduction. Pay particular attention that the last note of the generated voice with the cantus in one measure does not create parallel fifths with the interval on the first beat of the next measure.
- Direct (hidden) fifths and octaves are forbidden. A direct fifth or octave occurs when two voices move in similar motion (both ascending or both descending) into a perfect fifth or octave, without at least one voice arriving by step. To avoid this, use contrary motion instead in the generated voice.
- Voice crossing: Permitted only as an exceptional measure when no other voice-leading solution is possible. To be valid, the crossing must be resolved within three beats by leaping out to restore the original vocal register.
- Excessive spacing is forbidden. The interval between the cantus firmus and the counterpoint must not exceed a tenth at any sounding moment. This is a hard limit that applies to every single note, including passing tones and tied notes. When the cantus firmus rises to its highest pitches, the counterpoint below must rise accordingly to stay within a tenth. For example, if the CF reaches A4, the counterpoint below must be no lower than F3 (A4 to F3 = M10). Plan ahead: if the CF ascends, the counterpoint must ascend with it. Failing to track the CF's register is the most common cause of spacing violations. The constraint applies without exception to beat 3 (the preparation note of a syncopation). At beat 1, a single exception applies: in a consonant syncopation, if the tied note forms a consonant interval against the new cantus firmus note but that interval exceeds a 10th (up to a 12th), this wider spacing is tolerated provided the counterpoint immediately leaps on the following note to restore spacing within a 10th.
- Leaps into or out of unison are forbidden, except at the beginning and the end.
- Immediate pitch repetition is forbidden. A pitch may not be repeated on consecutive beats. The sole exception occurs in the ornamental resolution of a syncopation, where a repeated pitch between beats 2 and 3 may appear as part of the prescribed ornamental figure.
X. Summary Checklist for Generation and Verification
For each note written, verify the following in order:
- Species identification: Is the active species logic (1st, 2nd, 3rd, or 4th) correctly determined by the metric position and rhythmic value?
- Beat 1 consonance: Is beat 1 consonant? If dissonant, is it a valid fourth-species suspension tied from beat 3 of the previous measure?
- Beat 3 consonance: Is beat 3 consonant? A half note on beat 3 MUST be consonant (it follows second-species logic). If dissonant, does it satisfy the third-species four-note scalar rule (unidirectional stepwise motion with consonant beats 2 and 4)? A dissonant half note on beat 3 is NEVER permitted — only a dissonant quarter note on beat 3 can be valid (as part of a four-quarter-note passage).
- Passing dissonance: Is every dissonance on beats 2 or 4 approached and left by step in the same direction?
- Suspension mechanism: Does every dissonant suspension follow the three-phase sequence (consonant preparation, suspension, resolution by descending step)? Verify that the PREPARATION is consonant — if the half note on beat 3 that gets tied is itself dissonant against the CF, the syncopation is invalid and must be reworked.
- Admissible suspensions: Are suspension intervals limited to 7-6, 4-3, 9-8 (above) or 2-3, 4-5, 9-10 (below)? Is the 9-8 / 2-1 restriction on preparation interval respected? When writing below the CF, remember that the resolution is always DOWNWARD and the interval number INCREASES (2→3, 4→5, 9→10).
- Eighth-note usage: Are eighth notes used only as ornamented accent or ornamented anticipation within a syncopation context? No groupings of four eighth notes?
- Parallel fifths and octaves: Does the reduction (removing ties and diminutions) reveal any parallel or hidden fifths or octaves?
- Voice crossing: If a crossing occurs, is it resolved within three beats?
- Spacing: Does every note stay within a 10th? In particular, does beat 3 (the preparation) stay within a 10th without exception? If a consonant syncopation produces a 12th on beat 1, does the counterpoint immediately leap back within a 10th on the following note? When the CF is high and the counterpoint is below, verify that the counterpoint has risen high enough.
- P4 check (below CF): For every note in the counterpoint below the CF, verify that the interval from the counterpoint up to the CF is NOT a perfect 4th. A P4 is dissonant even when the counterpoint is the lower voice. If you find a P4, replace it with a consonance (3rd, 5th, 6th, 8ve, or 10th).
- Melodic conduct: Is every melodic interval singable? Is every leap followed by stepwise contrary motion? Are melodic tritones (direct or outlined) absent?
- Pitch repetition: Is immediate pitch repetition avoided (except for ornamental syncopation figures)?
- Rhythmic variety: Does the exercise use a mix of rhythmic values without excessive repetition of the same pattern?
- Cadence: Does the penultimate measure use a 7–6 suspension resolving to an octave (above) or a 2–3 suspension resolving to a unison (below)? Is the cadential accidental applied correctly to the resolution note (D/C#, G/F#, A/G#)?
- Opening: Does the first sounding note form a perfect consonance (unison, octave, or fifth if above)?
- Closing: Is the final measure a single whole note forming a perfect consonance (unison or octave)?
- Bb Rule: If the CF is in F, is B written as Bb wherever it creates a melodic tritone with F (direct leap or stepwise outline)?
Please check at the end if all those rules are correct and if not correct yourself.
